# Spymaster: the field, and where we stand

Research notes from 2026-11-25. Sources are linked at the end.

## Is anything like it out there?

Not quite. No game on Steam (or the other catalogues checked) puts a language model across the table as your
opponent in a spy game. LLM deception exists only as research: models bluffing each other at Diplomacy or Coup.
Those are benchmarks, not games.

The three closest games by theme:

| Game | What it is | Scale | How its opponent works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sigma Theory: Global Cold War** (2019, Mostly Positive, ~890 reviews) | Run an intelligence agency in a near-future cold war: recruit agents, seduce, blackmail, steal research | Hours-long campaign against several nations | Scripted rival nations |
| **Phantom Doctrine** (2018, Mostly Positive, ~4,700 reviews) | Cold War tactics in the style of XCOM, with a base, a roster of agents and an investigation board; double agents and brainwashing | Long campaign; tactical combat at its core | Scripted enemy organisation |
| **Espiocracy** (unreleased; Steam lists 2026–2027) | Cold War grand strategy: one of 74 countries' agencies; coups, operations, operatives who can be turned | Very large; 34 operation types | Rules-based world AI |

Reviews of these games repeat the same praise and the same regret. Critics credit Phantom Doctrine with coming
closer than anything else to a real spy simulator, and wish for more games built around spycraft itself. They
also find its many interlocking systems poorly explained. Players report Sigma Theory turning repetitive after a
few hours. The appetite for spycraft is there; complexity and length are what get in the way.

## How Spymaster is different

1. **The opponent reasons in language, and can be fooled.** Claude reads the same fog of war you do, judges
   people (is that report bait, is my own agent turned), and writes down why. Plant the right lie and it will
   execute its own loyal agent. Every rival above follows rules.
2. **Loyalty is the whole game.** No tactical combat and no economy. The only question is who is really
   working for whom, and it cuts both ways: either side can turn the other's agents and run them as moles.
3. **Thirty minutes, one duel.** Twelve weeks, six to ten agents, four or five orders a week.
4. **The ending tells the story.** An AI-written debrief shows both sides' reasoning, the turning point and
   where each side went wrong. It's the moment people want to share.

## Positioning: bite-sized, not sprawling

Brawl Stars took the best of a sprawling genre (MOBAs like League of Legends) and condensed it: fewer
heroes, short matches, instantly readable, endlessly replayable. Spymaster can do the same for espionage
strategy, which today means Phantom Doctrine's tactics and Espiocracy's grand strategy.

- **The pitch:** a spy duel you can finish over a coffee, against an opponent that actually thinks.
- **Keep:** one map, one opponent, a handful of agents, one question (who is still yours?), a finish in
  half an hour, a story at the end.
- **Refuse:** campaigns, base building, economies, tech trees, tactical combat. Each is a reason the
  competitors run long and explain poorly.
- **What makes it spread:** the debrief and highlight pages ("here's what the AI was thinking while I played")
  are the hook for social sharing; a daily challenge on a shared seed would let people compare games.
- **Two audiences, one game:** plain language (Spymaster) for everyone; Smiley mode (Moscow Rules) as the
  find for le Carré fans.

## Raising the visual bar (concepts, not copies)

Sigma Theory's agents are painted, near-photographic faces in a saturated purple world of glowing rings
and orbs. What makes them memorable is that **each agent's face is distinctive and shows up in the same
place every time.** Playtest 3 found our agents hard to remember. These ideas keep our noir, minimal
look and stay fast (no full-screen characters):

1. **Faces you can tell apart at token size.** Give each agent one strong, consistent visual trait
   (spectacles, a hat, a beard, a scarf, grey hair), with an outline that reads at 24px, and keep exactly that
   face on the map, in the City list, in reports and in the debrief.
2. **A personal colour.** A thin ring tint per agent within each side's palette, so "the one in mustard"
   becomes a memory hook alongside the name.
3. **An agent card on hover.** A larger portrait, the role badge, and what you know or suspect: a dossier
   corner, not a screen.
4. **A living map.** The morning plays out on the map before the reports: a pulse where someone died, a
   flash where a trap sprang, a soft glow on cities where something happened overnight. Lamplight and
   night between weeks.
5. **One signature element.** Sigma Theory has its glowing orb. Ours could be the dossier stamp or the gold
   lighter's flame: one recurring, polished motif used sparingly.

Suggested order: 1 and 3 together (they answer the playtest note directly), then 4, then 2 and 5.
